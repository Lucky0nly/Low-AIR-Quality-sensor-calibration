ML PROJECT ANALYSIS DOCUMENT
Low-Cost Air-Quality Sensor Calibration Using Machine Learning
1. Dataset Selection and Description
1.1 Overview
The proposed research focuses on the automated calibration and error compensation of low-cost particulate matter (PM2.5) sensors against regulatory-grade reference instruments using Machine Learning (ML). Low-cost optical particle counters (OPCs) and light-scattering sensors (such as Plantower PMS5003 and Honeywell HPMA115C0) have enabled dense spatial air-quality monitoring networks. However, their raw measurements suffer from significant systematic errors, baseline calibration drift, particle size truncation, and severe environmental confounding—most notably aerosol hygroscopic growth under elevated relative humidity (RH > 70%) and ambient temperature fluctuations.
To establish a defensible, reproducible, and robust calibration framework, multi-parameter time-stamped datasets containing co-located low-cost sensor telemetry and regulatory reference measurements (e.g., Beta Attenuation Monitors [BAM] or gravimetric equivalent monitors) are required.
Three candidate datasets were identified and evaluated for model development, feature ablation, and cross-domain generalization. These datasets represent distinct geographical topographies, meteorological regimes, aerosol compositions, sampling frequencies, and concentration ranges (from clean European urban levels to severe seasonal pollution episodes in the Indo-Gangetic Plain).
1.2 Dataset 1: PurpleAir Sensor Network in Urban and Rural India
Dataset Link / Permanent DOI: https://doi.org/10.6078/D1RQ70
Source: University of California, Berkeley / Dryad Open Data Repository (Campmier et al., 2023; Atmospheric Measurement Techniques)
License: Creative Commons Zero (CC0 1.0 Universal - Public Domain)
Data Collection Period: July 2018 to January 2021 (Multi-year, cross-seasonal deployment)
Co-location Sites:
• Delhi (Urban, North India): July 2018 – April 2020
• Hamirpur (Rural, Himachal Pradesh): January 2020 – January 2021
• Bengaluru (Urban, South India): June 2019 – August 2020
Description:
This benchmark dataset contains continuous, long-term field co-location records of low-cost PurpleAir PA-II monitors deployed adjacent to regulatory-grade reference instruments across three climatically contrasting regions of India. The PurpleAir PA-II houses dual Plantower PMS5003 optical particle sensors (channels A and B), providing redundant raw count and mass concentration channels alongside onboard temperature and relative humidity telemetry. Regulatory ground-truth target measurements are acquired from federally approved Beta Attenuation Monitors (Met One BAM 1020 and BAM 1022).
The dataset captures extreme dynamic concentration ranges (PM2.5 spanning 5 μg/m³ to >500 μg/m³) across severe monsoon, post-monsoon crop residue burning, and winter inversion conditions, making it an ideal testbed for evaluating non-linear ML calibration and environmental feature interactions.

1.3 Dataset 2: Q-SCALE Low-Cost Sensor Calibration Benchmark Dataset
Dataset Link / Permanent DOI: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.11068317
Source: CERN Zenodo Open Data Repository (Chiavassa, Giusto, & Bergadano, 2024; Politecnico di Torino)
License: Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC-BY-4.0)
Data Collection Period: November 2022 to June 2023 (8 consecutive months)
Co-location Site: ARPA Piemonte official regulatory air quality station (Torino Rubino), Turin, Italy
Description:
The Q-SCALE dataset provides high-resolution, multi-sensor telemetry recorded by six identical custom low-cost air quality monitoring stations deployed directly alongside an official European regulatory monitoring station. Each low-cost unit integrates four independent Honeywell HPMA115C0-003 optical particulate matter sensors, a calibrated DHT22 temperature and relative humidity sensor, and a BME280 barometric pressure transducer.
Ground truth is provided by ARPA Piemonte's official regulatory instruments (combining beta-attenuation and gravimetric reference methods). Telemetry is available at 1-minute and 1-hour median aggregations under an open CC-BY-4.0 license, providing a rigorously controlled European urban benchmark with moderate concentration ranges (2 μg/m³ to 120 μg/m³).

1.6 Dataset Selection Strategy
Datasets 1 (PurpleAir India) and 2 (Q-SCALE Italy) are selected as the primary two-dataset benchmark for all experimental pipelines. Dataset 3 provides the reference parameterization for the EPA linear baseline model.
Before model training, rigorous exploratory data analysis (EDA) and quality control are applied:
• Range Validation: Screening physically impossible sensor values (PM2.5 < 0 μg/m³ or >1000 μg/m³; RH < 0% or >100%; T < −40°C or >60°C).
• Channel Consistency: For PurpleAir, channel A and B readings are cross-checked; records with channel divergence exceeding 5 μg/m³ and 20% relative difference are flagged as hardware clogs.
• Temporal Partitioning: Chronological splitting (60% training, 20% validation, 20% testing) is enforced strictly without shuffling to ensure future temporal dynamics do not leak into training sets.
2. LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Introduction
Accurate measurement of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is critical for epidemiological assessments, air quality compliance, and public health protection. While regulatory monitoring stations utilizing Federal Reference Methods (FRM) provide gold-standard accuracy, their capital costs ($30,000 – $100,000) and infrastructure requirements severely restrict spatial coverage. Low-cost optical particle sensors ($50 – $300) have democratized air monitoring; however, their light-scattering readings are non-specific and susceptible to environmental confounding, particularly particle swelling due to water uptake under high relative humidity (RH > 70%) and temperature-dependent optoelectronic drift.
Traditional calibration methods relied on multiple linear regression (MLR) and empirical humidity growth curves (such as Köhler theory approximations). Over the past four years (2021–2024), research has shifted toward non-linear Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) architectures. These include Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Regression (SVR), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks.
The following systematic review synthesizes 16 peer-reviewed journal papers published in prominent atmospheric and environmental engineering journals between 2021 and 2024. It evaluates their experimental settings, modeling approaches, key findings, and documented limitations to establish a sound foundation for the proposed research.

2.3 Critical Analysis of the Literature
2.3.1 Evolution of Calibration Methodologies
The literature demonstrates a clear trajectory from static linear calibrations toward non-linear machine learning ensembles. Early operational standards, such as the EPA national correction (Barkjohn et al., 2021), applied multiple linear regression with additive RH terms. While computationally trivial, linear models cannot represent the exponential physics of water condensation described by Köhler theory. Studies from 2023–2024 (Raheja et al., 2023; Seng et al., 2024) confirm that tree-based ensembles (Random Forest, XGBoost) consistently achieve superior calibration fidelity (R² > 0.90, RMSE reductions > 40%) on tabular telemetry without requiring the excessive training resources or hyperparameter volatility of deep neural networks.
2.3.2 Emphasis on Regression Accuracy over Error Decomposition
Most existing studies assess calibration purely through summary correlation metrics (R², RMSE, MAE). While these metrics reflect overall regression fit, they obscure operational failure modes:
• How does calibration bias behave under extreme humidity (RH > 85%)?
• Does the model introduce negative concentration predictions during clean periods?
• Does the algorithm preserve high-concentration pollution peaks or artificially compress dynamic range?
Answering these questions requires rigorous residual analysis partitioned across concentration and humidity strata.
2.3.3 Environmental Confounding and Hygroscopic Growth
Optical particle sensors estimate mass concentration by measuring light scattering from particles drawn into a detection chamber. Below ≈ 50% RH, aerosol particles remain relatively dry, and light scattering scales predictably with geometric diameter. Above 70% RH, deliquescence occurs: soluble aerosol components (sulfates, nitrates, sea salt) absorb ambient water vapor, causing exponential particle diameter growth. Because light scattering scales approximately with particle diameter to the sixth power in the Rayleigh regime and second power in the Mie regime, water uptake causes massive overestimation of dry PM2.5 mass. Accounting for this requires models capable of learning sharp non-linear interaction thresholds between raw particulate counts and ambient humidity.
2.3.4 Generalization Across Climatic Regions, Seasons, and Sensors
The reviewed literature reveals an overwhelming reliance on single-site co-locations. When models trained in one environment are evaluated elsewhere, performance frequently collapses. For example, Raheja et al. (2023) showed that US EPA models failed completely in West Africa due to differences in aerosol size distributions and hygroscopicity. Evaluating calibration architectures across two independent datasets representing contrasting continents (India vs. Italy) provides crucial empirical evidence regarding algorithm generalizability.
2.3.5 Sensor Degradation, Drift, and Temporal Leakage
Optical sensors experience laser diode decay, optical chamber contamination, and internal fan speed degradation over time (Curto et al., 2023). Many published studies inadvertently inflate model performance by utilizing randomized K-fold cross-validation on time-series telemetry. Because consecutive sensor readings are autocorrelated, random splitting leaks past and future temporal context into the test set. Strict chronological splitting (training on historical data, testing on future unseen periods) is mandatory for realistic evaluation.
2.3.6 Feature Ablation, Explainability, and Edge Readiness
While environmental covariates (T, RH) are universally recognized as important, the literature exhibits a critical gap: very few studies conduct controlled feature ablation to quantify exactly how much accuracy is gained by adding environmental features to raw sensor counts across different algorithms. Furthermore, complex deep learning architectures act as uninterpretable black boxes. Deploying lightweight, explainable classical ML models (Random Forest, SVR, XGBoost) offers transparent feature attribution and execution speeds suitable for real-time microcontrollers.
2.4 Identified Research Gap
Candidate Research Gap: Although tree-based machine learning methods (Random Forest, XGBoost) have demonstrated superior accuracy over linear models in isolated local studies, there is a critical lack of standardized, reproducible benchmarks that simultaneously evaluate Random Forest, SVR, and XGBoost across two independent public datasets representing contrasting climatic and concentration regimes, while explicitly quantifying: 1. The marginal accuracy contribution of environmental covariates through controlled feature ablation (Sensor-Only vs. Sensor + T + RH), 2. Temporal stability under strict forward-chronological validation splits, and 3. Systematic residual behavior across severe relative humidity and pollution strata.
2.5 Proposed Research Direction
• Reproducible Multi-Dataset Benchmark: Implement and evaluate Random Forest, SVR, and XGBoost against raw sensor and linear baselines on two open datasets (PurpleAir India and Q-SCALE Italy) under identical preprocessing.
• Systematic Feature Ablation: Train parallel model variants using (a) raw PM2.5 sensor inputs alone, and (b) raw PM2.5 combined with ambient temperature, relative humidity, and engineered interaction terms (PM × RH) to quantify the exact value of meteorological features.
• Strict Chronological Evaluation: Enforce a forward 60/20/20 train/validation/test split on time-sorted records to evaluate true temporal generalization without data leakage.
• Stratified Residual Analysis: Quantify model bias, error variance, and hygroscopic compensation across binned humidity brackets (<50%, 50–70%, 70–85%, >85%) and concentration ranges.
• Computational Overhead Assessment: Measure model training duration, memory footprint, and inference latency per sample across all models, providing clear engineering guidelines for edge deployment on low-power IoT microcontrollers vs. cloud architectures.
2.6 Conclusion
The academic literature indicates that machine learning is essential for overcoming the physical limitations of low-cost optical particulate monitors. However, moving the field forward requires moving beyond single-site demonstrations. A rigorous, reproducible comparison of established classical ML algorithms across two geographically independent public datasets, coupled with controlled feature ablation and stratified residual analysis, addresses documented shortcomings in the literature and provides actionable guidelines for real-world environmental monitoring networks.

Method
Related Published Research
Purpose
Raw Sensor Baseline
Standard operational benchmark
Measures uncalibrated low-cost optical performance, establishing the initial error baseline.
Multiple Linear Regression (MLR)
Barkjohn et al. (2021), AMT; Nilson et al. (2022), AMT
Represents standard regulatory baseline (US EPA equation formulation) incorporating linear T and RH terms.
Random Forest Regression
deSouza et al. (2022), AMT; Wang et al. (2023), Atmos. Environ.
Captures non-linear sensor-hygroscopic interactions through bagged decision trees; resilient to noise and outliers.
Support Vector Regression (SVR)
Villanueva et al. (2023), Sensors; Seng et al. (2024), STOTEN
Maps input features into high-dimensional space via Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel with ε-insensitive loss.
XGBoost Regression
Raheja et al. (2023), EST; Seng et al. (2024), STOTEN
Iterative gradient-boosted decision trees optimizing regularized loss; state-of-the-art accuracy for tabular telemetry.
Implementation Procedure:
The datasets are cleaned, standardized, and partitioned chronologically:
Dtrain (60%),    Dval (20%),    Dtest (20%)
For feature ablation, two distinct feature spaces are constructed:
Sensor-Only Feature Vector:
xsensor = [ PM2.5_raw ]
Sensor + Environmental Feature Vector:
xenv = [ PM2.5_raw,   T,   RH,   PM2.5_raw × RH,   hour,   month ]
Target variable y is the co-located regulatory reference measurement (PM2.5_ref).
Preprocessing transformations (StandardScaler for SVR) are fitted strictly on Dtrain and applied forward to Dval and Dtest. Hyperparameters (tree depth, number of estimators, learning rate, SVR C and γ) are tuned on Dval via 3-split TimeSeriesSplit cross-validation. Models are evaluated using Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Coefficient of Determination (R²), and Mean Bias Error (MBE).
5. TENTATIVE RESEARCH CONTRIBUTIONS
• Standardized Two-Dataset Calibration Benchmark: Deliver an open, reproducible comparative benchmark implementing Random Forest, SVR, and XGBoost across two independent public datasets (PurpleAir India and Q-SCALE Italy) spanning extreme vs. moderate pollution regimes and distinct sensor hardware.
• Quantitative Environmental Feature Ablation: Provide an empirical ablation study quantifying the exact marginal accuracy gain (ΔMAE, ΔR²) achieved by incorporating temperature and relative humidity across each algorithm, establishing whether tree ensembles or kernel methods benefit more from meteorological inputs.
• Rigorous Chronological Generalization Analysis: Evaluate temporal calibration stability over forward test horizons without temporal data leakage, identifying degradation rates across multi-month deployment windows.
• Hygroscopic and Concentration Stratification: Characterize residual distributions and bias profiles across four humidity intervals (<50%, 50–70%, 70–85%, >85%) to verify whether machine learning effectively eliminates the exponential overestimation caused by deliquescence.
• Practical Computational Feasibility Assessment: Quantify training time, memory consumption, and inference latency per sample across all models, providing clear engineering guidelines for edge deployment on low-power IoT microcontrollers vs. cloud architectures.
